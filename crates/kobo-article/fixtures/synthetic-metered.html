The woman who winds the town clock

By Halima Osei

The clock in the tower above the corn exchange at Marlow Cross has been wrong, by the reckoning of the people who live under it, for exactly eleven days in the last forty years, and Odette Farrow can tell you the date and the cause of every one of them.

She has wound it by hand every Monday and Thursday since the spring of 1986, when the council's caretaker retired and nobody else in the building could be persuaded to climb the ladder. The movement was made in 1871 and has never been fitted with a motor. It wants ninety turns of a crank the length of a forearm, and it wants them from somebody who does not mind pigeons.

Farrow was a bookkeeper at the exchange then, and she took the key because she was tired of hearing the argument about who should. She did not expect to keep it. She is seventy-three now, and the ladder has been replaced twice, and she has outlasted four council clerks who each began by suggesting that the job might be done by a contractor.

"It is not hard," she told me, on the Thursday I went up with her. "It is only regular. People hear that as the same thing as hard."

"And if you are ill?"

"Then I am ill on a Tuesday."

Eleven days

The log of the clock's failures is kept in a ledger of the kind she used downstairs, ruled for pounds, shillings and pence. Six of the eleven days belong to the winter of 1991, when the oil in the going train thickened in the cold and the hands stood at ten past four for most of a week. Two were a power cut, which stopped nothing in the clock but put out the lamp she winds by. One was a wedding.

  • 1991: six days, thickened oil in the going train.
  • 1998 and 2003: a day each, the lamp out and the ladder not safe in the dark.
  • 2009: one day, her daughter's wedding, by arrangement with the mayor.

The remaining two days she will not discuss, beyond saying that they were in the same week and that the clerk responsible no longer works for the council. The ledger records them in red.

A clock that is right is not noticed. That is the whole of the job, to not be noticed twice a week.

What the town thinks of this arrangement is hard to establish, because the town mostly does not know about it. I asked nine people in the market square who kept the clock, and seven said the council, one said it was electric, and one, a man selling eggs, said it was Mrs Farrow and that he set his watch by her.

She has trained nobody to follow her. There was a boy from the grammar school in the nineties who came up for a term and then discovered motorbikes. The council has a line in its budget for an automatic winder, carried forward unspent each year since 2004, and she has an understanding with the treasurer that it will go on being carried forward.

We came down at twenty to six. In the square the hands stood where she had left them, a minute fast, which is how she likes them on a market day, so that the buses are caught.
