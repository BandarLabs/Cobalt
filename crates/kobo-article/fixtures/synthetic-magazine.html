The last lighthouse keepers

The lights went automatic decades ago. A handful of people still look after them.

By Ruth Adeyemi

June 2, 2026

18 minutes

Every Tuesday morning, whatever the weather, Elspeth Gray drives eleven miles along a single track road to the lighthouse at Skerry Point, unlocks the door at the foot of the tower and climbs one hundred and fourteen steps to check that the light is still turning.

It always is. The light has been run by a computer in a cabinet since 1989, and a fault would send an alarm to an office two hundred miles away long before Elspeth noticed anything. But the cabinet cannot see a cracked pane, a gull's nest in a vent, or the slow creep of rust along a railing, and so, for a small fee and out of something closer to love, she goes and looks.

"The machine keeps the light. I keep the lighthouse."

There are perhaps forty people like her around the coast, attendants rather than keepers, a word the last generation of keepers is careful about. A keeper lived at the light, trimmed the wick, wound the clockwork and kept the log. An attendant visits.

A log that was never closed

The keepers' logbooks are kept in an archive in the city now, but at Skerry Point the last one stayed in the watch room, and Elspeth writes in it every week. Her entries sit under those of the men who lived here, in the same columns, recording the same things: wind, visibility, the state of the light.

She showed me the page where the handwriting changes. The last keeper's final entry is three words long: Light handed over. The next line, in a different hand and a different decade, notes a broken catch on the lantern door.

What the attendants do

The work is mostly looking, and writing down what was seen. Elspeth's checklist, handed to her by the lighthouse board when she started, has not changed in twenty years:

  1. Check the lantern glazing for cracks and salt.
  2. Check the vents and gutters for nests and blockages.
  3. Record the state of the paintwork on the tower and the railings.
  4. Sign the log.

None of it is complicated. All of it, she says, would be done by nobody if she stopped.

When I asked whether she worried about who would come after her, she looked out at the water for a long time before she answered. Then she said that somebody always had, so far, and went back down the hundred and fourteen steps to lock the door.
